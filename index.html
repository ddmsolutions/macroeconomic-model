The read

Growth held up better than expected through the first half, but some of June's strength was one-off (World Cup, heatwave), and Q3 now carries the full effect of oil above $100. The OBR's March forecast predates the conflict, so its 2.3% inflation call for 2026 is already stale; the MPC now expects CPI slightly above 4% in 2027 Q1.

The policy tension is clear: a loosening labour market argues for patience, while three MPC members want to lean against second-round effects. Markets price the first hike by December. For the public finances, higher debt interest and benefit uprating are pushing borrowing above forecast ahead of the Autumn Budget.

Outlook to 2030

The central path blends three things: the estimated model's own forecast, a no-change benchmark where that beat the model in testing, and the official and consensus forecasts (OBR, Bank of England, Treasury survey), with Bank Rate anchored on market pricing. Q3 2026 GDP is pinned to a nowcast from July GDP and the September PMI. Shaded bands show the 50% and 90% ranges, taken from the actual spread of the model's past forecast errors. They are lopsided where history is: GDP has a long downside tail from 2008.

How the central path is built

RPI central = CPI central plus the modelled RPI wedge from the basket below. Its range reuses CPI's error bands.

Model assumptions: Brent averages $91 in Q3 2026 then stays near $100; sterling flat at $1.345; the Ofgem cap rises 13% in July and 4% in October, then holds. Bank Rate is 3.75% through Q3, and its anchor is the market path (about 4.2% by mid-2027; 2028 to 2030 extrapolated as a gently falling curve, my assumption). Q3 GDP growth is set to the 0.5% nowcast. The blend weights are the ones that minimised past errors (GDP 50% model, CPI 75%, Bank Rate 50%, unemployment 0%). The official anchor carries 50% weight to 2028 and 75% after that, beyond the range the model was tested on. Borrowing and debt are fiscal years (2026 = 2026-27).

RPI on its own basket

RPI is built up from its 2026 basket weights: 961 parts per 1,000 cover goods and services priced much as in CPI, plus RPI-only housing items (house depreciation 75/1000, council tax) and the Carli averaging formula, which together are modelled as a wedge over CPI that drifts back to its long-run 0.7pp. Mortgage interest payments (39/1000) follow Bank Rate with the lag of fixed-rate refinancing, so they keep adding to RPI even as rates fall (the equation starts 1.5pp below its fitted value to match the latest ONS reading, fading over two years). From February 2030 RPI takes CPIH's methods and prices, so the wedge fades month by month through 2030 (CPIH assumed 0.2pp above CPI). RPI still drives index-linked gilts, many regulated fares, some student loan interest and older pension and commercial contracts.

Fiscal rules in 2029-30

Stability rule: the current budget (excluding net investment) in surplus in 2029-30. Investment rule: net financial liabilities falling as a share of GDP in 2029-30, approximated here by net debt. Headroom is the starting margin (set under Fiscal levers) less the scenario's effect. Also affected: frozen tax thresholds (receipts rise faster than pay), cash-limited departmental budgets to 2028-29, benefits uprated with last September's CPI, RPI-linked gilts and the speed at which higher rates reach debt interest.

How sure is this scenario?

Reruns the scenario 120 times, varying the uncertain parts: how strongly rates and fiscal policy move demand, energy and sterling pass-through, how far pay reacts to high inflation, the market links, and the size of every outside shock you have set (±30%; policy levers such as tax, spending and Bank Rate are taken as given). Long run to 2040

What the current scenario leaves behind once the business cycle has passed. The quarterly model runs to 2030; after that, demand effects fade and only supply-side changes persist: productivity, migration, people returning from long-term sickness, public and private capital, and the drag from higher debt on investment.

Scenario versus central, selected years

How the long run is built

  • Baseline growth after 2030 is about 1.25% a year: productivity 1.2% a year (midway between the OBR's 1% medium-term and 1.4% long-run assumptions), plus hours growth of about 0.05% (ONS 2024-based projections: population up about 0.2% a year, less roughly 0.15 points for ageing, my estimate).
  • Persisting effects: productivity levers continue each year; migration keeps adding workers (and people, so GDP per head is shown too); public investment builds a public capital stock worth 0.1% of GDP a year per 1% of GDP invested, depreciating 3% a year; business tax relief raises the capital stock as in the OBR's full-expensing estimate; each point of extra debt lowers output about 0.08% through higher borrowing costs.
  • Debt: unchanged policy from the 2030 position, with ageing adding about 0.2% of GDP a year to spending (health, state pension and social care, a straight-line reading of the OBR's 2026 long-run projections), 3.8% effective interest and 2% inflation. The scenario keeps its lasting borrowing change, pays interest on its extra debt, and gains 0.1 points of GDP of primary balance per 1% of extra output from productivity or capital (spending partly rises with the economy); migration's fiscal effect is held at its 2030 level, since the OBR finds migrants' net contribution shrinks as they age. On these assumptions central debt edges down until the mid-2030s, whereas the OBR's 2026 long-run projections have it rising from 2032-33, so this baseline is on the kinder side.
  • This is a projection under stated assumptions, not a forecast. Beyond 2030 nothing has been tested against data.

Party platforms

What each party's latest stated tax, spending, trade and migration plans would do in this model, as if in force from 2027. The next general election is due by August 2029, so this is a hypothetical test of the policies, not a forecast of any election. Every party is treated by the same rules.

Differences from the central path unless stated. GDP is the level by 2030. "Half delivered" applies the same test to every party: the savings or revenue that pay for their pledges raise only half what the party says, while the pledges themselves go ahead in full.

How platforms are mapped

  • Costings are the party's own where published, converted at about £32bn per 1% of GDP. Tax changes go to the household tax lever, day-to-day spending and welfare to the spending lever, capital spending to public investment.
  • Labour is the central path, which reflects current government policy as official forecasts do.
  • Conservative: October 2025 platform (£47bn of named savings; over £20bn of tax cuts including stamp duty abolition, which is assumed to lift house prices about 2%).
  • Reform UK: September 2026 conference platform (£15,000 personal allowance; £80bn of savings and migrant levies).
  • Liberal Democrat: 2024 manifesto, the party's latest full costing (£27bn of tax rises, £26.8bn of day-to-day spending, £19.7bn a year of borrowed investment, closer EU trade).
  • Green: 2024 manifesto, the latest full costing, plus 2026 statements (wealth tax, £8.4bn energy bill support, rejoining the EU). The £90bn carbon tax is modelled like a consumption tax, so it raises prices once.
  • No party has published a numerical net migration target (Institute for Government, October 2025), so migration is left at the central path for all. Use the migration levers to explore.

What the model cannot judge

  • It is a demand-side model over five years. Spending increases lift growth and cuts reduce it in the short run. It has no effects of taxes on incentives to work, invest or relocate, no gains or losses in public-service productivity, and no effects after 2030. That favours high-spending platforms and penalises consolidation in the short run, whatever their long-run merits.
  • Market confidence enters only through the automatic gilt premium, which rises with debt. Sudden loss of confidence of the kind seen in 2022 is not built in; use the gilt stress lever.
  • Not modelled: nationalisation, rent controls, leaving the ECHR, deportation targets, net zero rules, regulation, and the distribution of gains and losses between households.
  • Deliverability varies widely. The IFS judged the Green revenue unlikely, the Lib Dem revenue at risk, and Reform's savings to need large unspecified cuts; the Institute for Government called the Conservative welfare savings unprecedented.

Out-of-sample errors by horizon

RPI: given actual CPI and Bank Rate, the RPI build-up tracked 2022 to 2026 with a typical error of 0.75pp, running slightly high in 2024 when mortgage refixing slowed. The mortgage interest equation explains 83% of the variation since 2011.

The model forecast unemployment worse than simply assuming no change, so the central path uses no change for unemployment. The model is still used for how unemployment responds in scenarios.

The 2022 back-test

Starting from end-2021 data, and told the actual path of oil, household energy bills and sterling, the model predicts CPI peaking at 7.5% against an actual 10.7%. It captures about half the surge. It misses food prices (food inflation peaked at about 19%), the wage response, and the Energy Price Guarantee's timing. With energy held flat it predicts almost nothing, as nearly every forecaster did.

It also under-predicts how far the Bank of England raised rates, because the estimated rule reacts gradually. Treat energy-shock scenarios as the lower end of the plausible inflation response.

Pay, food and services equations (added September 2026)

Quarterly, 2001 to 2026 (the pay series starts in 2001), Covid years excluded. Pay is AWE regular pay growth (KAI9); food and services are CPI annual rates (D7G8, D7NN).

What the new blocks add, and what they do not

Pay. Pay growth barely responds to inflation below about 4%, but above that it responds strongly: each point of inflation above 4% adds about 0.16 points to pay growth each quarter, building to more than a point. That threshold is identified only from 2022 to 2023, the one high-inflation episode in the sample. Rising long-term sickness lifts pay (fewer workers), and slack lowers it.

Used for scenarios only. The pay loop changes how scenarios differ from the central path; the central path itself does not use it. An out-of-sample test (2006 to 2025, re-estimated each quarter) of a simpler version, with a straight-line inflation term and pay added to the inflation equation, cut the two-year CPI error only slightly (1.21 to 1.18 points), and that pay equation forecast worse than assuming no change (1.54 against 1.45 points). So the central pay path stays on the official anchor. The above-4% version could not be tested this way, because its only evidence is the period it would be tested on.

Food. Food inflation follows oil two quarters earlier, household energy bills, sterling and pay (R² 0.91). Once energy is in the inflation equation, food adds no further forecasting power for headline CPI, so energy's effect on food is not counted twice: the separate food channel only carries world food price shocks you set.

Sterling. A trade-weighted index is now built from 11 currencies (euro 48%, dollar 21%, renminbi 7% and others). Its link to rate changes is estimated (1.6% per point, t = 2.9), but pass-through to prices cannot be pinned down in the data with either the dollar or the trade-weighted rate, so it is set at 0.2 (a 10% fall adds about 2% to prices over two years), the middle of published UK estimates.

Estimated coefficients

Greyed t-stats are not statistically significant. The Phillips curve slope (unemployment gap) is close to zero in UK data since 1993, so demand shocks move inflation very little in this model. That is consistent with the widely reported flattening of the UK Phillips curve, but it may understate inflation risk in a tight labour market.

Estimated versus calibrated

Estimated from UK data (1993–2026, Covid excluded): inflation persistence and pass-through from oil and household energy bills; output-gap dynamics; the Bank Rate reaction function (long-run responses 1.5 to inflation and 0.75 to the output gap, strong smoothing); the link from growth to unemployment.

Calibrated to published estimates, because the data do not identify them well: the effect of interest rates on demand (set so a sustained 1pp rise in Bank Rate lowers GDP by roughly 0.6% after two years, in line with Bank of England estimates); sterling pass-through (10% depreciation adds about 1% to prices, since the only sterling data available here is against the dollar); fiscal multipliers (OBR-style: spending 0.6, tax 0.35, public investment 1.0); oil's real-income drag; migration, tariffs, the fiscal block; the business-lending spread, expectations, world-demand and investment links. The sterling, gilt-yield, QE, house-price and saving links are estimated; bank losses are calibrated to the 2025 stress test. The out-of-sample tests above cover the central forecast, which the links do not change; the links only shape scenarios.

Data: ONS GDP (IHYQ), CPI (D7G7, with the index chained from it), unemployment (MGSX) and household energy CPIH (L56Z); Bank of England Bank Rate history; monthly Brent and dollar exchange rates. The output gap and neutral rates are statistical trend estimates, which are least reliable at the end of the sample.

Method and sources

How the model works

A quarterly semi-structural model with four estimated equations and a calibrated fiscal and business block. It runs from 2026 Q3 to 2030 Q4 and is aggregated to calendar years. Scenarios are computed as the model's response relative to its own baseline, then added to the central path.

  • Output gap gap = 1.58·gap₋₁ − 0.69·gap₋₂ − 0.07·(real Bank Rate − neutral)₋₂ − 0.003·oil y/y₋₁ + demand shocks. Demand shocks are scaled so their long-run effect equals the multiplier listed under Accuracy.
  • Inflation (quarterly, seasonally adjusted) π = anchor + 0.40·(π₋₁ − anchor) + 0.21·(π₋₂ − anchor) + 0.010·Δoil + 0.048·Δbills + sterling + one-offs. The anchor is the 2% target, shifted by the expectations lever. Household bills follow wholesale energy at half strength with a two-quarter lag, reflecting the Ofgem cap.
  • Overheating Once unemployment falls below the sustainable rate (a lever, 4.5% by default), inflation gets an extra push that grows with the square of the shortfall. Unemployment 1pp below adds roughly 1pp to annual inflation once it has built up; 2pp below adds about 4pp. UK data since 1993 point the same way but cannot pin the size down (t-statistic about 1, with only one tight episode, 2022, which the energy shock also muddies), so the strength is set between the estimate and more cautious published views.
  • Bank Rate i = 0.93·i₋₁ + 0.107·π + 0.055·gap + 0.057·r* + c, estimated outside the zero-bound years. It ignores VAT and energy-support one-offs. The MPC stance scales the inflation response.
  • Unemployment Δu = 0.39·Δu₋₁ − 0.066·(g − g*) − 0.097·(g − g*)₋₁
  • GDP growth potential growth (about 1% a year, statistical trend) plus the change in the gap, plus productivity and labour-supply levers.
  • Automatic links (on by default, switchable). Each responds to the scenario's gap from the central path, and any lever for the same variable adds on top. Estimates use UK data 1993 to 2026 excluding Covid, with robust standard errors.
    • Sterling rises 2.3% per 1pp rise in Bank Rate relative to the central path (estimated, t = 2.9) and falls 4% per 100bp of extra UK gilt risk premium (calibrated to September 2022).
    • Gilt yields rise 5bp per point of net debt (estimated from ONS debt and 10-year gilt yields: t = 1.9 in four-quarter changes, 3.4 in levels). QE holdings lower yields by about 12bp per 1% of GDP held (t = 2.7 to 3.6), consistent with the Bank's estimate that the first £200bn cut gilt yields by about 100bp.
    • House prices follow the estimated dynamics on the Bank of England two-year fixed mortgage rate: a sustained 1pp rise takes about 3.4% off prices (t = 3.3), with momentum carrying moves forward.
    • Household saving rises 0.37pp for each 1pp rise in unemployment over a year, building to about 1.3pp before fading (estimated on the ONS saving ratio, t = 2.7).
    • Bank lending spreads widen 20bp per 1% the economy falls below the central path, capped at 400bp. Mortgage data show no independent widening once rate cuts are allowed for: the apparent widening in 2009 was fixed-rate mortgages lagging Bank Rate cuts (a 1pp cut widens the spread by 0.17pp at first, t = 5.5). So this link is a modest, calibrated allowance for business and unsecured lending, where spreads did widen in 2008 but no data was available here.
    • Inflation expectations drift up by 4% of any excess in underlying inflation each quarter and fade slowly; the stance lever scales the drift (calibrated).
    • World demand falls 0.4% per 10% rise in oil prices (calibrated; falls in oil do not boost it).
    • Business investment moves 2.5% per 1% of output gap and adds capacity after six quarters; only the capacity effect is added (calibrated).
  • Borrowing and debt use the annual fiscal block: automatic stabilisers of 0.5, debt interest of 0.12·i + 0.06·Σgilt + 0.1·π (matching the OBR's £15bn per 1pp), and productivity effects matching the OBR's range.
  • Crisis mechanics (levers under "Crisis mechanics"):
    • Lower bound and QE. Bank Rate stops at a floor you set (0.1% by default; negative allowed). When the rule wants to go lower, QE buys enough gilts to take about 40bp off yields per 1pp of missing cuts (80bp if aggressive, capped at 200bp). That matches the 2009 programme: roughly 4pp of cuts the Bank could not make, £200bn of purchases, and about 100bp off yields. QE's effect on demand is set at 1% of GDP per 100bp, below the 1.6pp average GDP effect of QE1 across 16 studies reported by the Bank, to be cautious.
    • Debt deflation. A price level below the path borrowers expected cuts spending by 0.1% of GDP per 1% shortfall, doubled once prices fall outright. It is scaled to today's household debt of 117% of disposable income (Q1 2026, against 156% at the 2008 peak); calibrated.
    • Bank failures. Bank capital losses are calibrated to the Bank of England's 2025 stress test, where unemployment peaking at 8.5%, house prices down 28% and GDP down 5% took the banks' aggregate CET1 ratio from 14.5% to 11.0%, a 3.5pp drawdown still above regulatory minima and buffers. So losses are 0.41pp of capital per point of extra unemployment and 0.066pp per 1% fall in house prices. "Today's" buffers absorb 3.5pp before lending is rationed, "stronger" 5pp, and "2008-era" only 1pp. Beyond that, spreads widen up to 300bp more and investment is cut. A government rescue halves the crunch but adds its cost to borrowing.
  • Policy levers added for business and migration
    • Business investment tax relief is scaled to the OBR's assessment of permanent full expensing: about 0.3% of GDP a year in cost (£9 to 11bn), business investment up about 1.2% a year, and the capital stock 0.5% higher in the long run. Per 1% of GDP of relief: investment up 4% and capacity up about 0.1% a year from 2028.
    • Government loan guarantees cut SME lending spreads by 50bp per 1% of GDP guaranteed, with expected losses of 10% a year of the stock added to borrowing. Both numbers are judgement; I found no UK estimate of how much guarantees lower spreads.
    • Migration is split into work and family migration and international students. Each 100,000 people adds about 0.14% to GDP a year through labour supply; students count at 30% because most work part-time. Students' fees and spending are exports, about £100,000 per student over a course (HEPI, 2026 cohort), which the model counts as 0.25% of GDP per 100,000 arrivals a year once numbers have built up.
    • Housing: each 1 percentage point change in the immigrant share of the population moves house prices about 0.8% and rents about 0.75% (Migration Advisory Committee review, June 2026: median 0.65% and mean 1% for house prices; 0.64% to 0.87% for rents). Rents feed CPI through their 8.4% weight.
    • Staff shortages: cutting work migration adds 0.15pp a year to pay growth per 100,000, reflecting shortages in care, health and hospitality where non-UK workers are heavily represented, and 0.05% of GDP a year to public spending for NHS and care pay (judgement).
    • Fiscal effect of migration is calibrated to the OBR's March 2024 scenario, where 200,000 a year lower net migration raised debt by 3.1% of GDP after five years without offsetting spending cuts.
  • Business lens pay follows the estimated pay equation; SME loan rates add any credit-spread shock; sector weights are judgement.
  • Added in September 2026
    • Pay, food and services are estimated equations (see Accuracy). Pay feeds back into inflation (0.043 per point of extra pay growth each quarter) and responds strongly once inflation is above 4%. Food carries world food price shocks; energy's effect on food is already in the inflation equation.
    • Market expectations. Two-year fixed mortgage rates and sterling respond to the expected path of Bank Rate over the next two years, and the 10-year gilt to the expected path over ten years, taken from the scenario's own rate path (the model is rerun until the path it expects is the path it gets). A hike that markets already price is in the central path; only surprises move things.
    • Trade and the current account. Exports follow world demand, tariffs, trade-weighted sterling (price elasticity about 0.13, consistent with the demand block) and productivity gains; imports rise 1.2% per 1% of GDP and 0.2% per 1% sterling appreciation; a 10% rise in oil costs about 0.1% of GDP on the energy trade balance. Elasticities are calibrated; ONS export and import volume series were not reachable for estimation.
    • Long-term sickness. 100,000 fewer people inactive through ill health adds about 0.15% to GDP (70% relative productivity and hours), saves about 0.045% of GDP a year (£15,000 each in benefits and tax, OBR 2023) and lowers pay pressure through the estimated inactivity term.
    • Fiscal rules. Stability and investment rule headroom in 2029-30, from the OBR's March 2026 figures (£23.6bn and £27.1bn) or September independent estimates for the stability rule (about £10bn; the investment rule is assumed to lose the same £13.6bn, as no estimate was found). Debt interest: 0.21% of GDP per point on short rates in 2026-27, falling to 0.11% by 2030-31 as QT shrinks reserves (OBR ready reckoner), plus about 4.2% interest on any extra debt, plus gilt refinancing at 0.06% a year per point, plus 0.25% of GDP per point of RPI on index-linked gilts (a quarter of the stock). Frozen thresholds: receipts rise with pay at an elasticity of about 1.2. Departmental budgets fixed in cash to 2028-29, with half of any real squeeze from higher inflation assumed to stick.
    • Uncertainty. 120 reruns drawing the interest-rate effect on demand (±35%), demand multipliers (±25%), oil and energy pass-through (their standard errors), pay's response to high inflation, sterling pass-through, the market links, and the size of outside shocks (±30%; policy levers are not varied). The range covers parameter and judgement uncertainty, not the chance of new shocks.
    • Nowcast. Q3 2026 growth set at 0.5%: July GDP carried forward gives about 0.6%, the September flash PMI of 51.7 about 0.3% (rule of thumb), weighted two-thirds to one-third.
